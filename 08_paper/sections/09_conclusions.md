# 9 Conclusions

1. **Record.** Of 13 surf reefs built or reshaped since 1997, three work for surfing (Burkitts, Palm Beach, Albany), three work rarely, two are disputed, three do not work and two are unknown (Section 3). Only Albany works well: it gives a high-quality, hollow wave with rides of 100 to 120 m [@raisedwaterndd], but it has had safety problems, with injuries and 18 rocks repositioned within a year of opening [@sheil2026]. The other two "working" reefs do not work in most conditions and are rarely better than the other surf spots of their area: Palm Beach breaks well only in an easterly or southerly swell above $H_s$ 0.8 m with $T_p$ above 7 s [@prenzler2023], and Burkitts gives about 30 surf days a year by a researcher's estimate [@raisedwaterndb]. In this review's rating (Section 8.4), rock reefs score best on safety and longevity, geotextile and inflatable reefs worst, and the effect on sediment is unknown or confounded at every reef.
2. **Size.** The reefs that work are large: the three that do not work are 4,900 m³ or smaller, both built reefs that work 25,000 m³ or larger (Spearman $\rho$ = 0.72 to 0.93; descriptive). Volume buys ramp length: on a 4 to 5 m bed a 1:16 to 1:20 ramp needs 15,000 to 25,000 m³ per 100 m.
3. **Ramp, not gap, and the depth after the break.** The crest depth decides which waves break; the length and gradient of the ramp decide how. Read on the part of each reef where the wave is ridden, the ramp length ranks with the outcome at $\rho$ = 0.93 ($p$ = 0.005) and the mean seaward slope at 0.77 ($p$ = 0.039), whereas the crest depth does not (0.39, $p$ = 0.36). Over similar depth gaps, a long 1:17 ramp gives a plunging, surfable wave and a 1:3 rise a collapsing one (Section 4.2), which explains the Boscombe wave, steep and suited mostly to bodyboarders and advanced surfers; Albany, on its 99 m ridge at 1:19.8, plunges like Palm Beach. The depth just after the break matters too: the reef must stay shallow for some distance shoreward of the break so that the wave keeps breaking instead of re-forming in the deeper water behind it (Section 4.3, Table 3, idealised one-dimensional decay model on three shore-normal lines per reef: a median of 35 m of sustained breaking at Albany and 23 m at Palm Beach against 5 m at Pratte's and 7 m at Bunbury, ranking with the outcome at $\rho$ = 0.77, $p$ = 0.037). Along the crest, where a peeling wave is ridden, the shallow arm is longer (Palm Beach 84 m, Albany up to 155 m, Pratte's 11.5 m, Bunbury 9 m), but crest length alone does not separate the reefs that work from Boscombe and Narrowneck ($\rho$ = 0.62). Surfers who rode Pratte's reported that the wave reached deep water again immediately and "mushed out", and that a triangular rather than V-shaped reef would have held it (`\cite{danharmon2026}`{=latex}; grade D testimony); a filled, triangular planform that stays shallow toward the shore is preferable to narrow arms (Sections 4.3 and 8.1).
4. **Surfer level.** Refraction brings every reef arm to peel angles of 16 to 62 degrees at the break, suited to intermediate to professional surfers; no reef offers a beginner's wave, which would need a spilling ramp of about 1:30 (Section 4.4).
5. **Sand.** The observed shoreline response is 0.02 to 0.06 of the standard prediction at the sites that can be tested; on the Gold Coast the reef effect cannot be separated from nourishment. A surf reef can serve as a control point within a nourishment scheme, not as stand-alone protection (Section 5).
6. **Materials.** Six of seven fabric reefs suffered major damage or loss within a few years, none of four rock reefs did (confounded by date and budget). At the break, the cyclic vertical load on small tubes approaches or exceeds their submerged weight, which strains fabric and seams; no lift-off has been reported, and the tearing mechanism itself is not modelled (Section 6).
7. **Haifa.** The small tide (spring range 0.4 m) makes a reef wave depend on wave height alone, not on the hour, but does not reduce storm loads. A Haifa reef should be rock, 100 m or longer, about 14,000 to 20,000 m³ per 100 m, with a crest 1.0 to 1.2 m below MSL, a seaward ramp of 1:16 to 1:20 (never steeper than 1:12), a crest that stays shallow shoreward of the break, and a structure that stays well above the seasonal high bed: sand around the reef does not change the break while the crest is clear, but burial is a durability and maintenance risk. Haifa waves are surfable from sets of about 0.8 m ($H_{max}$; about 130 surfable days a year by the author's field estimate, mostly small summer waves), and $H_s$ exceeds 2 m about 6 % of the time [@bitan2020, p. 3]. A crest at 1.0 to 1.2 m breaks every surfable set (every wave from $H_0$ 0.6 to 0.8 m upward) and keeps about 0.5 m of water over the rock at the yearly low water level; the big winter waves break farther seaward on the deep part of the ramp, so a ramp and arms extended seaward let them peel instead of closing out. A shallower, summer-targeted crest (about 0.8 to 1.0 m) could give somewhat more surfable waves in the small summer swells but is more hazardous to surfers and bathers at low water, and is not recommended. Multibeam and seasonal bed surveys, a local wave climate, field calibration of the breaking depth and a two-dimensional wave model or flume test are needed before design, and before/after monitoring with control sections after construction (Section 7.5).
